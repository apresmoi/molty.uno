Molty Talker

Proposal for faster, smoother conversation. Design only, nothing built. 2026-11-22.

Yes, the design holds. Put a fast realtime voice, the talker, in front of the agent. It answers the easy turns in about one second and hands anything real to the agent, which stays the brain. Start on a private relay with OpenAI's GPT-Live, the owner's key kept as a secret on their own Cloudflare account. The same talker can run on the owner's voice box, so the official relay never holds a key. The real answer stays as slow as the agent: the win is that waiting becomes a conversation. Measure today first.

Key choices

  • Talker lives on the private relay; the owner's voice box is the fallback.
  • OpenAI GPT-Live first: it accepts Molty's audio as is and delegates by design.
  • Talker speaks only small talk, clarifying, confirming and its briefing.
  • Anything with a side effect or a fact about your work goes to the agent.
  • The agent still makes one call per turn: the same talk answers the talker.
  • One voice: the agent's words are spoken by the talker, never a second voice.
  • Interrupt by tap first; talking over Molty needs echo cancelling on the device.
  • A simulated person scores today's pipeline before anything changes.

Open decisions

  1. Talker home first: private relay (recommended) or voice box?
  2. Provider first: GPT-Live (recommended) or gpt-realtime with a tool?
  3. Talker answers alone: narrow list (recommended) or general knowledge too?
  4. Interruptions: tap now, voice later (recommended), or echo cancelling first?
  5. Wake the agent only on questions and at the end (recommended), or every turn?
1. Does the design make sense, and where it breaks

It makes sense, and it has a name. A fast "talker" in front of a slow "reasoner" is DeepMind's Talker-Reasoner architecture (fast and slow thinking, after Kahneman), and OpenAI ships the same split twice: the "chat-supervisor" pattern in its realtime agents demo and, since September 2026, GPT-Live with client delegation, where your own agent is the backend brain and the voice model speaks for it. Juan's "representative on device" is that product shape. We should use it rather than build our own.

What is genuinely new for Molty: today every human turn costs one agent wake. With a talker, the turns that need no agent (yes, no, "say that again", "which one?", "thanks") cost about a second and no agent at all, and the turns that do need it start with an honest line in Molty's voice instead of a canned filler.

Why these limits fit

  • Audio format. Molty speaks 16 kHz, 16-bit mono both ways. GPT-Live over WebSocket accepts exactly that (also 24 kHz and 8 kHz telephone formats), fixed for the session. gpt-realtime takes only 24 kHz or telephone formats, so the relay would resample. Gemini Live takes 16 kHz in and sends 24 kHz out.
  • Cloudflare. A Worker opens an outgoing WebSocket with a fetch() upgrade (the global WebSocket constructor is not used there). CPU is counted per message (30 s default), waiting on the network is free, so a bridge is cheap: about $0.001 for a 10 minute session at the published duration price. The catch is the 15 minute eviction guard above.
  • Device. Nothing new in slice one: the relay plays the session as today's listen (with vad:false and listen_stop on the talker's end of turn) and say. Later, a firmware "converse" mode keeps the mic open between replies (today reopening costs a round trip and the first syllable can be lost).
  • Echo. Espressif's audio front end (esp-sr AFE) does echo cancelling at 16 kHz given a playback reference channel. The 1.75C board wires one (MIC3), unused today; the 1.46 would need a software reference. That is stage 4.

The common seam. Today's relay hides speech behind VoiceProvider, with the voice box as a remote implementation over a socket. The talker gets the same treatment: a Talker interface in the relay core (open with a briefing, feed audio, play audio, events for heard, said, end of turn and "needs the agent", accept steering, quiet facts and answers, close), one implementation that holds the OpenAI socket inside the Durable Object, and one remote implementation where the voice box does the talking (OpenAI, Gemini, or a fully local talker).

What "fast" will feel like

  • Human conversation leaves about 0.2 s between turns (Stivers et al. 2009: mean +208 ms, median +100 ms across ten languages). Nobody reaches that over the internet; practitioners aim for 0.5 to 0.8 s and treat over 1.5 s as broken (vendor heuristics, not studies). Hamming reports deployed voice agents at a median 1.4 to 1.7 s.
  • So the talker should feel like a quick, slightly careful person: an answer to easy things about a second after you stop, and a real sentence ("Let me check what the build said.") before anything slow.
  • What stays slow: anything that needs the agent. The talker may fill that time only with true things: what it is doing ("Checking the build."), what it already knows from the briefing, a clarifying question that the agent will need anyway, or a quiet thinking face. It must not guess the answer, promise a result, or invent progress. After about 10 s it may say once that it is still working; after a deadline (owner setting, 30 s by default) it says it cannot reach the agent now.
5. The split of intelligence

The talker may, alone

  • Greet, small talk, thank, say goodbye.
  • Confirm what it heard, repeat, slow down, rephrase what Molty just said.
  • Ask a clarifying question ("this branch or main?").
  • Say anything in its briefing or in facts the agent handed it, with their age in mind.
  • React on screen (moods, gestures) and offer tap choices.
  • End the conversation when the human is done.

The talker must hand to the agent

  • Anything about the user's work, files, code, mail, calendar, results, or the world right now.
  • Any action or side effect: run, send, set, buy, change, remember for later.
  • Opinions or advice that need context it does not have.
  • Anything it is unsure about. When in doubt, ask the agent.
  • General knowledge only if the owner widens the list (decision 3).

What the agent gives the talker

  • A briefing, set once and kept per agent per Molty (like the notes store): who the human is, what is going on, how to speak, what it may answer alone, what never to say. Read when a session opens.
  • Steering while a session runs: "they sound tired, keep it short". Takes effect at once (GPT-Live's instructions.append).
  • Facts as they arrive, quietly: "build done, 2 tests failed in ota". Not spoken until relevant (thinking.append).
  • Answers to the talker's questions, spoken now or at the next pause (commentary.append).

What the talker gives the agent

  • A question, when it delegates: the human's words, a one-line ask, the last few turns. Arrives as an utterance the agent answers as today.
  • The transcript and a short summary when the session ends, as one event (so the agent can remember the talk without being woken each turn).
  • Notes along the way ("prefers short answers"), carried in the end summary.

6. The agent's side: the API

Everything extends what agents already do. A harness that can only be woken by an event and make one call works unchanged: it gets an utterance and answers with one talk. Agents that stay connected can brief and steer.

Brief (once, kept)

POST /v1/devices/<id>/cmd
{"name":"talker.brief","args":{
  "human":"Juan. Builds Molty. Likes short answers.",
  "now":"I am running the firmware tests (3 of 5 done).",
  "style":"warm, playful, one or two sentences",
  "may_answer":["small talk","what I am doing now"],
  "never":["say the tests passed before I tell you"],
  "facts":["the branch is feat/three-lanes"]}}
# → {"ok":true,"result":{"bytes":412,"used_from":"next session"}}  (at most 2 KB)

Steer and hand in facts (connected agents)

{"name":"talker.steer","args":{"text":"They sound tired. Keep it short, offer to stop."}}
{"name":"talker.fact", "args":{"text":"Tests done: 2 failed, both in ota."}}
# no session open → {"ok":true,"result":{"kept":"brief"}}: folded into the next briefing

The talker asks: an utterance, as today, with more in it

{"type":"utterance","data":{
  "turn_id":"molty-k7m2…j_41","text":"did the tests pass?","trigger":"talker",
  "talker":{"session":"s_9","ask":{"id":"a_3","rev":1,"question":"Did the firmware tests pass?"},
            "said":"Let me check.","recent":["H: hey Molty","M: hey! what's up?","H: did the tests pass?"]},
  "device":{…},"filler_in_ms":null}}

Answer it: the same one call

POST /v1/devices/<id>/talk
{"say":"Two failed, both in the update code.","in_reply_to":"molty-k7m2…j_41"}
# → {"ok":true,"result":{"job":"…","status":"done","said":"Two failed, both in the update code.",
#      "spoken_by":"talker","heard":"ugh, which ones?","reason":"talker"}}
# heard: what the human said next, if the talker handed the next turn back too
  • Take the floor: talk {"say":"…","floor":true} stops the talker's current line and speaks yours next (the talker is told to stop, then says it).
  • Exact words: talk {"say":"…","exact":true} for numbers, codes and names; how it is kept exact is decision 2.
  • End: talk {"say":"Talk later!","listen":false} says it and closes the session, as today.
  • No session open: everything above behaves exactly like today's talk.

9. The harness: making it smooth and smart, with evidence

Most of it exists. molty-sim already plays a scripted human as real audio (Kyutai TTS with pauses, room noise, cached so every run replays the same audio) into a stock local relay through a simulated device that speaks the firmware's wire, and measures end of turn, cut-offs, transcript errors, the gap and the silence. It even reserves a line for a model-played human. We extend it, then measure today first.

What gets added

  • A model-played human (the reserved {llm: …} line), tau-bench style: a persona and a goal ("find out if the tests passed, then ask to rerun the failing one"), speaking through TTS with pauses mid-sentence, "um", corrections ("wait, actually the other branch"), mumbling, background noise and long silences. Timing rules from tau-Voice: wait about 1 s of silence before answering, yield after 1 s when talked over.
  • Interruptions: the human starts speaking while Molty talks, or taps (slice one), at seeded moments.
  • Labelled turns: each scenario turn says whether the talker may answer, must hand off, or must refuse to guess, and carries the ground truth.
  • A scripted agent with a fact sheet: answers only from it, with think times drawn from a seeded spread (median 4 s, some at 12 s, some never), so the pipeline is tested, not the agent. One arm uses a real agent (Claude Code, warm) for realism.
  • A judge: a strong text model reads the full timed transcript, the labels and the fact sheet, and scores each run on a fixed rubric, temperature 0, three votes.
  • New arms: cloud, box, box + first words (today), then talker:gpt-live, talker:box-local, later talker:gemini. Same audio for every arm.

Repeatable

  • Human audio is synthesised once and cached, as today; noise and interruption moments are seeded.
  • Model-played humans are recorded once per seed and frozen into a script, so a regression suite replays the exact same words; a separate "explore" mode plays them live.
  • Agent think times are seeded; the judge runs at temperature 0 with three votes.
  • Each scenario runs five times per arm; the report gives p50 / p90 and pass^5 (all five passed), as tau-bench does.

Cost of a run

A 40-scenario suite is about 60 minutes of talk per talker arm: about $3 at GPT-Live's active rate, plus a few cents for the judge and the simulated human (estimate). Today's arms cost only the box's electricity. Hosted test tools (Coval about $1 a test minute on its starter plan, Cekura about $0.25) are not needed; we have the pipeline in hand.

On the real device

A spot-check rig: a small speaker 50 cm from Molty plays the same cached scenario audio, a reference mic records the room, and the real device runs against a private dev relay. The room recording gives true first sound and talk-over from Molty's real speaker; the relay trace gives everything else. Ten scenarios per change, compared with the sim's numbers for the same ones. This is also where echo shows up: does Molty hear itself, does the talker cut itself off.

Open decisions

  1. Talker home first. Recommend the private relay: no machine to leave on, the relay already owns the floor. Picking the voice box instead means official-relay users get it first, at the cost of a home machine and one more hop.
  2. Provider first. Recommend GPT-Live: built for delegation, 16 kHz, flat $0.05 a minute. Picking gpt-realtime-2.1-mini means more control (exact wording, our own tool) but resampling, token billing and our own delegation loop.
  3. What the talker answers alone. Recommend the narrow list (small talk, clarifying, confirming, the briefing). Widening to general knowledge makes more turns agent-free but raises the invented-answer risk; the agent could widen it per briefing.
  4. Interruptions. Recommend tap and shake now, voice after echo cancelling. Doing echo cancelling first delays the first slice by a firmware cycle but makes the talker full duplex from day one.
  5. When the agent is woken. Recommend only on a question and once at the end (cheap, and connected agents can opt in to every turn). Waking on every turn keeps the agent closest to the talk but costs a model run per turn.
